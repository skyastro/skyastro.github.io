Orrery Help

This web site enables users to visualize elements of the universe in four dimensions: fully rendering in space and time. The current version is defined in the version page and changes are noted in the release notes.

Animations

There are many animations in the orrery ranging from moons, to planetary system, the Solar System, the stars near the Sun, our Galaxy, nearby galaxies, and Abell clusters out to a significant fraction of the observable radius of the universe. The program starts out in the Solar System animation.
  • The Solar System Orrery shows major and minor components of the solar system. Positions of the the Sun, planets, asteroids and comets are displayed. Both the orbits and positions of the planets are shown. Moons are displayed in subanimations of each planetary system (double click on the planet or its orbit). The positions of more than 50,000 minor planets and comets are shown as dots. Data are taken from the Minor Planet Center of the Center for Astrophysics. Positions for these minor bodies for times more than a few years from the present are less accurate since we are using a current epoch orbit. Future upgrades may use update orbits appropriate for the time shown in the orrery. A few dozen spacecraft missions are also animated, but are generally only shown while the spacecraft are in interplanetary travel (e.g., between Earth and Mars but not during a Mars mission).

  • The Earth and environs shows the Earth, Moon and more than 20,000 objects orbiting the Earth that are being tracked by NORAD. The satellites are shown as dots and users can click to find particular objects as in the solar system. However since orbits of the satellites are strongly affected by the atmosphere, orbit manuevers and non-two body gravitational interactions, these orbits should not be extrapolated very far. However, while the detailed orbits of specific satellites may not be precise, the general characteristics of the distributions of satellite orbits remain accurate. Note that some attempt is made to shown only satellites that were launched before the 'current' time of the simulation, but this is done only with yearly granularity, and does not include objects which have reentered the atmosphere before the ephemeris time. So, e.g., neither Sputnik nor Spacelab are ever shown. The lighting of the Earth and Moon by the Sun is modeled and spheres show the features of both of these bodies. Double-clicking on the moon will change to a Lunar animation.
  • The Local Galactic Neighborhood renders the stars in the local Galactic neighborhood, within 100 parsecs, using the GAIA DR3 Nearby Stars Catalog to get positions and parallaxes. A parsec is 3.2 light years or 200,000 times the distance between the Earth and the Sun. It is comparable to the typical distance between stars and their nearest neighbors. Stars where the NASA Exoplanet Archive shows one or more planets are surrounded by colored rings. You can also highlight the path between any two stars (or a star and the Sun) and get the distance between them. For this feature you can show a path to stars that are beyond the 100 pc so long as SIMBAD has a measured parallax for the star. An animation showing the space motions of the stars is also available. You can show a vector while the simulation is running. E.g., you can see how the distance to Proxima Centauri is decreasing for the next few centuries. Since the 300,000 stars in this animation can tax graphics processors a smaller simulation based upon the Extended Hipparcos Catalog can be substituted by appending ?galaxy=small to the URL when starting this web site.
  • The Galaxy animation depicts the entire Milky Way. Four classes of objects are shown: Pulsars show the local structure of the Galaxy, though the more distant pulsars are mostly undetected. HII regions trace the disk but are also difficult to see throughout the Galaxy and the distance estimates are generally poor for using these to show detailed structure. Globular clusters are found in both the disk and bulge and should be reasonably complete except at low Galactic latitude. The same is true of companion galaxies. Two small sphere's are shown in the animation: yellow around the position of the sun (and roughly the size of the Local Galactic Neighborhood animation), and red at the center of the Galaxy. A diffuse halo is added for effect, but we do not add a diffuse disk due to the complex interaction between transparent components in the rendering. Pulsars and HII regions are shown as green and blue dots respectively. Globular clusters are shown as spheres twice the nominal radius of the cluster or at least 50 pc. Companion galaxies are shown as spheres with their nominal sizes. A few pulsars are found in some of the satellite galaxies.
  • The Nearby Galaxies animation shows the positions of galaxies within a few megaparsecs of the Milky Way. This color codes the types of galaxies. Where the disk orientation was available, the disks are [suppose to be] oriented appropriately. No time-dependent view is available -- while we have radial velocities for many of these galaxies we have only very rough ideas of the motion perpendicular to the line of sight. Users can still zoom and pan and use the same buttons as in the other animations and explore trajectories against the static framework.
  • The Abell Clusters animation shows Abell clusters with known redshifts (as of a few years ago). The Local Group and Virgo cluster are added for reference (with somewhat made up parameters). Note that clusters with low Galactic latitudes are obscured by our Galaxy which excludes a wedge ringing the Local Group.
  • Animations of the nine nine Planets show images and rotation of these bodies and their satellites.
  • Animations are also available for six moons , Earth's, the Galilean satellites of Jupiter, Titan, and Charon.

How it works.

Within each animation users can zoom and pan using gestures appropriate for the platform. E.g., a mouse drag does a rotation on a PC and the mouse wheel may be used to zoom. A shift/drag does a translation. On a touch screen device, single and two finger drags, and finger expands may be used. By default a control-click is needed to request information on the object under the mouse, but this can be toggled to all clicks.

To switch among animations use the three-dot menu, or double click on an object which has a sub-animation (e.g., planets with the Solar System, and some moons within their planetary system). You can also use the three-dot menu to find objects within the current simulation and if they have an associated animation you can click on the appropriate button.

If the center of the animation has not been moved, then zooming out far enough or zooming in close enough to the center will initiate a change in animations to the next appropriate scale.

Within any animation you can normally click on any corner of the animation to go to its base animation which is usually the animation in which the current Animation is embedded. E.g., if looking at the Titan animation, a double click in the corner will bring you to the Saturn animation. Another double click in the corner brings you to the Solar System animation, and one more gets to the Local Galactic Neighborhood, then the Galaxy, thence to the Nearby Galaxies, and finally to the largest scale animation, the Abell Clusters.

The animation controls on the top left allow you to stop/start, reverse and change the speed of the animation.

The three-button menu allows you to set a specific time, look for objects by name, select among the primary animations, and view help and other capabilites. Just click on the three-buttons to show or clear the menu. You can set the time of the current animation by entering a date as year number (possibly including a fraction), a Julian Day number, or an ISO formatted time (YYYY-MM-DD) to set the time of the simulation.

When entering text in boxes simply hit return to indicate that the text is complete.

You can display lists of objects in the current simulation either by clicking or by doing a search using the name search in the three-dot menu. Clicking shows under the mouse (or near it in the case of Earth satellites, or comets and asteroids in the Solar System). You can include * wildcards to find multiple objects in a name search, e.g., *Halley*, will return both the comet 1P/Halley and the asteroid Halley. Objects are shown directly below the three-dot menu. For each object in an object list you may have several options to click:

  • i: display information about the object
  • A: start the animation associated with this object (planets and some moons)
  • C: center the animation on this object. It will be displayed in the center of the display and you can now pan around it. Note however that this uses the current position of the object: it does not follow an object on an orbit, but you can repeatedly click on the button to do so. To restore the center you can set the center to be the Sun (or the planet if you are in a planet system animation)
  • O: Display the instantaneous orbit of the object. This does not include subsequent precession for moons or satellites. The orbit will persist in the simulation. For comets asteroids and artificial satellites this will also display a larger dot to represent the object.
  • D: display a for to generate a line showing connecting two objects. The line will be drawn whenever the Connect button is pressed down. You can press it repeatedly as the source and/or destination object moves. Only one delta is shown in the simulation at any given time. By default the line is drawn from the center of the animation to the selected object, but this can be changed by the user.

    When searching by name you search the components of the current simulation. If nothing is found a query is sent to SIMBAD to see if it knows both the position and distance to the object. Distances can be found as parallaxes for Galactic objects, and redshifts for extragalactic objects.

The Orbit Calculator

The Orbit Calculator enables a user who can give some elements of an orbit to calculate others. Fill in whatever information you have on the orbit and see if the full orbit can be determined. Note the distinction between elements that are constant along the orbit, and those that vary with time. If an orbit is fully defined in the orbit calculator the orbit can be shown in the Earth or Solar System simulations if the mass of the central body matches that of the Earth or Sun.

The Trajectory Calculator

The trajectory calculator is an experimental interface to the trajectory calculation capabilities of the orrery. These are explained in more detail in the trajectory scripting documentation.
